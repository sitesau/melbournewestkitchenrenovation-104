Two quotes for the same room can sit far apart because they are rarely pricing the same job. Pricing moves with cabinetry detail, benchtop material and whether the layout changes at all. Move a wall or shift plumbing and electrical points, and the scope pulls in demolition, rough-in work, joinery, wiring and plumbing at once. A refresh that keeps the current layout typically sits in a different cost bracket from a rebuild with custom joinery and structural changes.

That is why the first conversation should be about how the kitchen works now, not which benchtop looks best. For inner-west weatherboards, period homes and larger growth-corridor houses, layout questions tend to decide the money. Design, materials and lighting are planned before cabinetry is made, with drawings shown before work starts, so adjustments happen on paper rather than halfway through installation.

Line items to demand before you sign

A short lump-sum figure tells you almost nothing. An itemised, fixed-scope written quote shows where the budget sits before work begins and gives you a reference point if a variation is proposed later. Before accepting any figure, check the quote separately names:

  • demolition and removal of the existing kitchen
  • rough-in work for plumbing and electrical services
  • cabinetry, including door style and internal fittings
  • benchtop supply, templating and installation
  • splashback supply and fitting
  • plumbing and electrical trade work
  • appliance installation and final finishing

Also confirm that the drawings shown before manufacture are the exact version being priced. If the drawings and the quote describe different kitchens, the gap will surface later as a variation with a price attached.

Allowances versus fixed items

Not every number in a quote is final. Some lines are fixed in writing; others are allowance-based, meaning the price assumes a provisional figure that can shift once selections are made. Allowances are common for items chosen late, such as tapware or splashback tiles, but a low allowance can make one quote look cheaper than another without actually being so.

The practical test is to ask, for each line, whether the figure is fixed or provisional, and what happens to the total if your selection lands above it. A quote that survives those questions is worth far more than a lower one that does not.

For a lower-cost makeover, the same discipline applies in reverse. Ask exactly which existing elements stay in place and make sure they are named in the written scope. The cheaper option only stays cheaper if the retained cabinetry, benchtops or layout are protected on paper rather than assumed.

Payment stages, variations and the finish line

The structure of payments matters as much as the total. Check that the written quote sets out payment stages clearly and that the variations process is explained before signing. In Victoria, once domestic building work passes $10,000, a written major domestic building contract must be in place under the Domestic Building Contracts Act 1995, covering scope, price, payment stages and variations.

Projects should also run with the right permits and registered plumbing and electrical trades, consistent with Victorian building requirements. The current contract threshold should be confirmed with Consumer Affairs Victoria or the building regulator rather than assumed from an older project.

Timing shapes payment expectations too. A standard full renovation commonly runs 6 to 12 weeks from contract to handover, and fabrication, not demolition, usually holds the calendar. Cabinetry and benchtops often take 4 to 6 weeks off site, then around 2 to 4 weeks on site for demolition, installation, plumbing, electrical and finishing. If drawings or material choices are still moving, fabrication becomes the slow point; a settled design makes the on-site stage far easier to plan around a household's cooking routine. The finish line is handover of the working kitchen with warranty and compliance paperwork in writing, not the day the benchtop arrives.

Red flags when comparing quotes

When two or three written quotes land side by side, a short checklist separates the comparable from the decorative:

  • a single lump sum with no line breakdown
  • drawings that do not match the version being priced
  • allowance figures presented as final prices
  • a makeover scope that does not name which elements remain
  • no mention of how variations will be priced and approved
  • one headline date instead of a stage-by-stage timeline

A quick response after an enquiry is a service expectation, not evidence of fit. The stronger signal is a written quote that keeps answering questions as you read it line by line.

  1. Lock the brief. Decide whether the footprint stays or changes, and record how the kitchen works now, what must change and what can remain.
  2. Check the drawings. Review design, materials and lighting on paper before cabinetry is made, and confirm the drawings match the version being priced.
  3. Read the quote line by line. Separate fixed figures from allowances, confirm all trade work is included, and ask how variations will be priced and approved.
  4. Sign and track stages. Put the job under the correct written contract, confirm permits and registered trades, and treat written warranty and compliance paperwork at handover as the finish line.

Common questions

Why do two kitchen quotes for the same room differ so much? They often describe different jobs. Cabinetry detail, benchtop material and layout changes all move the price, and a lump-sum figure can hide what is included. Comparing itemised written quotes line by line, with drawings that match each version, shows where the real gap sits.

Is the cheapest makeover quote the safer option? Not automatically. A makeover only stays cheaper when the elements expected to remain are named in the written scope. If retained cabinetry, benchtops or services are assumed rather than specified, the saving can disappear the moment an unexpected replacement appears.

What should happen on the final day of the job? The working kitchen should be handed over with warranty and compliance paperwork provided in writing. For the household, that documentation, not benchtop delivery or the last trade visit, marks the practical end of the project.
